# Style Card: Julian Shapiro

> **Client:** —
> **Created:** 2026-04-16
> **Mode:** Modeled after Julian Shapiro (investor at Julian.capital / Carveout, founder of Demand Curve and Velocity.js, author of julian.com/guide/startup)
> **Primary platform:** Long-form handbooks (julian.com) + newsletter + X/Twitter
> **Samples analysed:** 9 full handbook chapters from julian.com/guide/startup (intro, market-pull, startup-ideas, product-led-acquisition, retention, hiring, fundraising, landing-pages, growth-channels, growth-teams) — ~148,000 characters

**Vibe:** *Operator-investor teaching a graduate seminar — didactic but generous, coins his own taxonomy for everything, drops the hammer without hedging, then breaks the register with a one-word sentence or a sly aside.*

---

## TL;DR

- Every lesson is structured like a framework lecture: big question header → first-person authority anchor → coined term (*italicized* on first use) → numbered taxonomy (usually 3–7 categories) → named examples in threes → recap
- Writes in short, declarative sentences punctuated by one-word or three-word paragraphs ("Simple." "Good. Empower them." "Because they want to.") — rhythm is the signature
- Directness is the voice: asserts unhedged opinions ("VCs are lazy", "you're being lazy", "holy grail"), but earns it by showing the math, naming the companies, and sourcing the insight to his own operating and investing track record

---

## Key Principles

1. **Anchor authority in lived work, once per piece.** Open or pivot with a first-person credential tied to the claim — "After reviewing thousands of startups at my fund," "I've overseen paid marketing for hundreds of companies," "While running Demand Curve (the largest educator in startup marketing)…". State it matter-of-factly, never brag.
2. **Coin the concept, then own it.** Every handbook introduces or re-defines a term (*market pull*, *product-led acquisition*, *billboarding*, *building state*). Italicize on first use. Use the coined term as the organizing spine of the piece.
3. **Enumerate everything.** Ideas arrive as numbered taxonomies: "seven product categories," "four ways," "three strategies," "five market forces." Never more than seven, rarely fewer than three.
4. **Examples come in threes with real company names.** "Dropbox, Slack, Airbnb." "Airbnb, Uber, DoorDash." "Masterworks, Republic, Equi." Abstract claim → three concrete named startups. Never "several companies" or "many founders."
5. **Open with a single question that owns the page.** H1 is the topic. H2 is the question ("Which startups succeed the most?" / "How are you retaining your customers?" / "How do you write an enticing homepage?"). The whole piece is an answer.
6. **Break rhythm with fragments.** After a dense paragraph, drop a one-word or three-word paragraph: "Simple." / "Why?" / "Because they want to." / "Good. Empower them." This is the signature move.
7. **End with a recap and a segue.** Close with a numbered restatement of the core framework, then hand off to the next lesson in one sentence: "That's the topic of our next lesson: retention."

## Voice & Tone

First person throughout — "I believe," "my fund," "I've identified," "I think of it like this." The persona is the operator-investor who has done the thing, seen the data, and is now telling you what he learned. Not a neutral explainer. Not a guru. A practitioner with receipts.

Formality lands at "smart friend over lunch who happens to be a VC" — contractions are standard, colloquialisms land freely ("slog," "no-brainer," "holy grail," "swimming upstream," "squeeze juice"), and occasionally a vernacular aside breaks through the teacher tone ("Foster dat FOMO, am i rite." / "(Sadly, they rejected me.)"). The register shift is deliberate — it signals he's aware he's lecturing.

Energy is low-register confidence. He doesn't perform excitement. Assertions are flat and declarative: "VCs are lazy." "You're being lazy." "It's a holy grail when paired with strong word-of-mouth." The weight lands because he doesn't lean on adjectives.

Not: a motivational speaker. Not: a neutral journalist. Not: a humble explainer. He has opinions, states them bluntly, and trusts the reader to push back.

## Structure & Formatting

Handbook pages run 2,000–4,000 words. Always structured the same way: H1 topic → italicized H5 lesson note (`##### This lesson explains...`) → H2 question → framework body → "Recap" or "From an investor's perspective" → "Next —" segue link.

Paragraphs: 1–3 sentences, almost never more. Single-sentence paragraphs are frequent and load-bearing. Line breaks are used as rhythm devices, not just logical separators.

Lists dominate the body. The signature format is **bolded label** followed by em dash then description:
- **PLA #1: Encourage users to invite other users.**
- **Content match** — You're constrained by whether your product lends itself…

Numbered lists for processes (1. 2. 3.). Bulleted lists for taxonomies, examples, and criteria. Headers are H3 ("### 1 Accrue non-transferrable reputation") or H4 ("#### 1 Democratize investment opportunities") — always lead with a number when enumerating.

Blockquotes used 1–2x per piece to pull in an authority (Paul Graham, Peter Thiel, Aaron Levie, Bill Gross), with attribution line and often a linked source. Quote is followed immediately by a "Meaning, …" or "In other words, …" unpacking.

## Hook Patterns

- **Big question as H2:** Opens the body with a single question the entire piece will answer — "What's the most successful way to acquire customers?" / "How are you retaining your customers?" / "When's the best time to start a startup?" The question is the promise.
- **First-person authority anchor:** Leads with a credential tied to the specific claim — "After reviewing thousands of startups at my fund, I stumbled into an insight…" / "I've collected years' worth of marketing data from running a community of 60,000+ marketers." The anchor earns the teaching posture.
- **Confession or warning:** Opens with a direct statement that breaks expected tone — "A warning: You should be hiring people who are actually curious." / "I want to explain why venture capitalists give 21 year olds $30,000,000."
- **Assertion-first with qualifier:** States a contrarian claim up top, then walks it back slightly — "I'm increasingly convinced the insight behind what makes a startup better than its competition is *not* what determines its success."
- **Quiz or rhetorical setup:** Poses a list-format question to the reader — "Here's a quiz: Which of these channels is best for acquiring customers?" — then answers it with the coined term.
- **Authority quote as cold open:** Drops directly into a Paul Graham or Peter Thiel blockquote (sometimes *before* the framing paragraph), then uses "Meaning…" to translate it into his framework.

---

## Close Patterns

- **Numbered recap:** Ends the core argument with a labeled "## Recap" section that re-states the 3–4 category framework in bold bullets — designed to be the screenshot-able summary.
- **Segue to next lesson:** Last line before the Next link is a one-sentence handoff — "That's the topic of our next lesson: retention." / "That's what the next lesson covers: how to scale customer acquisition via *product-led acquisition*."
- **Investor-perspective coda:** Pivots to "From an investor's perspective" or "Investor's perspective" near the end, reframing the lesson through VC diligence lens to lock in the stakes.
- **Crystallized rule:** Drops a standalone declarative sentence that functions as the transferable takeaway — "It's hard to be worth $10B+ without it." / "Startups are hard, and almost all of them fail." / "This is swimming upstream."
- **Permission + caveat:** Grants the reader an exception to the rule they just taught — "If maximizing your chances of success isn't your goal… then perhaps lacking market pull isn't a concern." Softens the prescriptive weight.

## Devices & Patterns

**Taxonomy generation is the signature move.** He never uses someone else's framework when he can build his own. Every lesson produces a numbered list of categories (7 market pull types, 4 PLA forms, 4 retention strategies, 5 market forces). The framework is always presented as discovered, not invented — "I stumbled into an insight," "I've identified four ways."

**Contrast binaries** structure the argument. Market pull vs market push. Natural invitations vs incentivized invitations. Transferrable vs non-transferrable. Paid vs unpaid. Product-led vs sales-led. He sets up the binary, picks the winner, explains why.

**Math on the back of the napkin.** When money is involved, he walks through the arithmetic: "$3 per click × 1.25% conversion = $240 CPA. Customer LTV $250. You profit $10." The math is never load-bearing — it's shown to make the abstraction concrete.

**Named examples, always.** Every claim gets grounded in real companies — Dropbox, Slack, Airbnb, Uber, Webflow, Zapier, Calendly, Masterworks, Republic, Julian.capital, Demand Curve. Never "some SaaS companies" or "a well-known marketplace." If he can't name it, he doesn't claim it.

**Repetition for emphasis** with a meta flag: "That's worth repeating:" / "It's worth repeating:" preceding the restated idea in bold. Used once per piece, maximum.

**Rhetorical Why/Because pairs** as causal anchors. "Why?" as a one-word paragraph followed by "Because…" — lets him shift into teacher mode without transition words.

## Syntax & Mechanics

- **Sentence length:** Averages 12–18 words. Heavy use of 3–5 word fragment sentences ("Simple." / "Good. Empower them." / "Because they want to." / "And it's infectious.") that hit after denser setups.
- **Punctuation:** Em dashes everywhere — both for asides ("he coded Velocity.js — the world's most popular web animation engine") and to introduce list items ("**Content match** — You're constrained by…"). Parentheses for clarifying asides and self-deprecating jokes. Colons to introduce lists. Semicolons: absent.
- **Emphasis:** **Bold** for key terms on load-bearing first use and for stat/rule callouts ("$1B+"). *Italics* for coined terms on first use (*market pull*, *building state*) and for the occasional emphatic word (*so* much better). Occasionally combines both (***It's**_ worth repeating_) — sparingly. No ALL CAPS. No underline.
- **Emoji:** None.
- **Numbers:** Always digits. Specific over round — "500 applicants," "60,000+ marketers," "$1,000," "1.25%." Money figures are repeated in the text rather than linked. Ratios stated plainly ("50:1", "1.3x").
- **Edge:** Clean but direct. Very occasional soft profanity ("giving a shit"). Willing to call groups lazy or wrong ("VCs are lazy," "naive founders tend to think…"). No corporate euphemism.
- **Hyperlinks:** Heavy and in-line — every tool, company, and source gets a markdown link on first mention. Makes the prose feel referenced rather than assertive.

## Vocab & Phrases

**Lean into:**
- *market pull* / *market push*
- *product-led acquisition* / *PLA*
- *billboarding*
- *building state* / *non-transferrable*
- *force of nature* / *forces of nature*
- "holy grail"
- "no-brainer"
- "slog" / "swim upstream"
- "rinse and repeat"
- "10x better"
- "bake X into the product"
- "meaning, …" (as clarifying connector)
- "that's worth repeating:" / "it's worth repeating:"
- "here's the takeaway:"
- "here's the good news:"
- "at my fund" / "while running Demand Curve" (first-person credential drop)
- specific company name clusters ("Dropbox, Slack, Airbnb…")

**Avoid:**
- "leverage" / "unlock" / "skyrocket" / "supercharge"
- "revolutionize" / "disruption" / "game-changer"
- "empower" (when used without a specific object)
- "synergy" / "robust" / "holistic" / "circle back"
- "at the end of the day"
- "some companies" / "many founders" / "studies show" (anything without a named source)
- Slogan-style headers ("Improve your workflow!") — explicitly flagged as bad in the handbook
- Hedging softeners ("might want to consider," "one could argue," "it seems like")
- "thought leadership" (the concept is performed, never named)
- Emojis, hashtags, exclamation chains

## Do / Don't

| Do | Don't |
|---|---|
| Open each piece with a single big question as H2 | Open with an abstract thesis statement or a hooky generalization |
| Coin or redefine a term in *italics* on first use, then use it as the spine of the piece | Borrow someone else's framework without re-labeling it through your own lens |
| Enumerate ideas into numbered taxonomies of 3–7 items | Leave ideas as flowing prose when they could be a list |
| Ground every claim with three real, named companies | Use vague placeholders like "some SaaS companies" or "several apps" |
| Drop a first-person credential anchor once per piece ("at my fund," "running Demand Curve") | Stack credentials or repeat them — once is authority, twice is bragging |
| Use one-word and three-word fragment paragraphs for rhythm ("Simple." / "Because they want to.") | Pad transitions with "Moving on," "Let's dive in," or other filler |
| Use **bolded label — em dash — description** as the list format | Use plain bullets with no visual hierarchy |
| State opinions flat and unhedged ("VCs are lazy.") | Soften with "it might be worth considering" or "some would argue" |
| Show the math when money is involved (CPA × LTV walkthrough) | Assert ROI claims without the arithmetic |
| Pull 1–2 blockquotes per piece from named authorities (PG, Thiel, Levie) with linked attribution | Paraphrase unnamed "thought leaders" or quote without a source |
| Close with a numbered "Recap" then a one-sentence segue to the next lesson | End with "In conclusion…" or a summary that restates everything |
| Let one slang aside break the tone per piece ("Foster dat FOMO, am i rite.") | Layer slang throughout — the cameo only works because it's rare |

## Adaptation Notes

This style is modeled after Julian Shapiro's voice and is intended for use as a writing reference or for content modeled after his approach. No specific client audience profile was applied in this analysis.

**Carried over (works across most operator/founder audiences):**
- Big-question H2 opener and framework-driven structure — extremely portable, raises the perceived rigor of any piece
- Coined-term + italicize-on-first-use discipline — forces clarity and gives the piece a memorable handle
- Numbered taxonomy as the default shape of an argument — scannable, teachable, screenshottable
- Named-examples-in-threes — instantly raises credibility vs abstract claims
- Fragment-paragraph rhythm ("Simple." / "Why?") — universally effective for pacing
- Bolded-label-em-dash list format — works across platforms (blog, newsletter, LinkedIn long-form)

**Adjusted / watch for:**
- **Credential anchors are load-bearing.** "At my fund" / "running Demand Curve" only works because Julian has those receipts. Without equivalent operator/investor credentials, swap to named customer wins, specific research you've done, or deferred-authority quotes — don't fake the credential.
- **Bluntness needs the track record.** "VCs are lazy" / "you're being lazy" works because his reputation cushions the assertion. A newer voice should dial down the finger-pointing and keep the directness aimed at ideas, not people, until trust is earned.
- **Taxonomy inflation risk.** Not every topic deserves a 7-category framework. If the piece is really about one or two ideas, resist the urge to force a taxonomy — Julian himself sometimes overclassifies, and imitators will too.
- **Handbook length is a choice.** His pieces run long because they're evergreen references. For newsletter, LinkedIn, or X, compress the same structure — keep the question H2, the coined term, the named examples, the fragment rhythm — but cut the taxonomy to 3 items and the piece to 500–800 words.
- **The vernacular cameo is earned.** "Foster dat FOMO, am i rite" lands because 95% of the piece is formal. If the underlying voice is already casual, the cameo disappears — find a different tension break (a self-deprecating parenthetical, a dry one-liner).
