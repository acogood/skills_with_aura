# Style Card: Julian Shapiro — Homepage Edition

> **Client:** —
> **Created:** 2026-04-16
> **Mode:** Modeled after Julian Shapiro, adapted for homepage conversion copy
> **Primary platform:** Homepage / Landing Page (above-the-fold → features → CTA)
> **Parent style card:** `writing-style-card-julian-shapiro.md`
> **Goal:** High conversion rate to signup

**Vibe:** *Operator telling a visitor exactly what you sell, who it's for, and why it beats the alternative — in the time it takes to scroll past the hero. Specific over clever. Direct over seductive. No taxonomies, no handbook lectures, no vernacular winks.*

---

## TL;DR

- Header must pass the litmus test: *if a visitor read only the hero text and nothing else, would they know exactly what you sell?* If no, rewrite it.
- One clear message per page, not a framework — drop the "7 categories" / "4 types" instinct; the hero picks ONE hook (bold claim OR top objection addressed) and the features deliver it
- Every feature is a **value prop + objection handled** paired with a bolded label and an em dash — scannable in 3 seconds, convincing in 15

---

## Key Principles

1. **Describe, don't sloganeer.** "Groceries delivered in 1 hour. Say goodbye to traffic, parking, and long lines." — not "Supercharge your grocery experience." If the header reads like a tagline, rewrite it as a description.
2. **Specificity is the conversion lever.** Specific numbers, specific alternatives, specific outcomes. "1/500th the price of a design agency" beats "affordable." "Design sites visually. No coding." beats "Easy site building."
3. **One message, not a framework.** The handbook instinct (enumerate 3–7 categories) kills homepage clarity. Hero = one value prop + one hook. Features = 3–6 proof points that ladder back to the same value prop. No coined term unless it's the product name.
4. **Every sentence earns its pixels.** Cut any word that isn't doing work. Short paragraphs, short sentences, fragment rhythm for scannability. "Visually design and develop sites from scratch. No coding." — that's the density target.
5. **Named social proof, always in threes.** Logo rows, customer counts, or "teams at Slack, Airbnb, and Dropbox use this." Never "trusted by industry leaders." Never "thousands of companies."
6. **CTAs continue the hero's promise.** If the header says "Groceries in 1 hour," the CTA says "Find food" — not "Get started." The button is the next step in the narrative, not a generic action.
7. **Show the math when you make a claim.** "$3/mo vs $60 for the cable bundle." "5-minute setup vs 2-week agency onboarding." Back-of-napkin arithmetic converts better than adjectives.
8. **Address the top objection in the hero, not the FAQ.** Whatever almost-stops-people-buying (usually: "but I can't code" / "but it's too expensive" / "but it'll take forever") goes in the subheader or the first feature — not buried below the fold.

---

## Voice & Tone

Plain-spoken practitioner-to-practitioner. Confident but not swaggering. Says what the product is, what it replaces, and who it's for. No performative excitement, no hype adjectives ("amazing," "powerful," "best-in-class"), no marketing poetry.

Formality sits at "smart founder explaining their product on a 3-minute Zoom demo" — contractions are standard, technical terms are fine when the audience expects them, and the voice stays declarative: "You do X. We handle Y. Here's how."

The big shift from Julian's handbook voice: **no vernacular cameos on the homepage.** "Foster dat FOMO, am i rite" works in a blog post because the reader has already decided they like you. A first-time visitor hasn't. Save the character moments for post-signup onboarding, emails, and blog.

**Not:** a guru. **Not:** a motivational speaker. **Not:** a teacher walking the visitor through a framework. The homepage is a description, not a lesson.

## Structure & Formatting

Use Julian's own landing page template from the guide:

1. **Navbar** — logo + 3–5 nav links, nothing more
2. **Hero** — header (10–14 words, descriptive) + subheader (1–2 sentences, 15–30 words max) + primary CTA
3. **Social proof** — logos of known customers/press, OR customer count if impressive ("Used by 10,000+ teams"). Immediately below the hero.
4. **Features & objections** — 3–6 features, each = bolded value-prop label + em dash + 1–3 sentence paragraph + image showing product in action. Each feature ties back to the hero's value prop.
5. **Repeat primary CTA** — same button copy, same narrative continuation
6. **Footer** — links, legal, secondary nav

Total length: the page can be long, but the *hero* must be complete in itself. Visitor decides in the hero; the features only need to close visitors who are already curious.

Paragraph length: hero subheader ≤2 sentences. Feature paragraphs ≤3 sentences. Anything longer splits into a second paragraph or a bullet.

## Hook Patterns (Hero)

Julian's guide explicitly defines two homepage hook types — use one, not both:

- **Bold specific claim:** Hero makes a concrete, specific, dopamine-triggering claim about the outcome. "Build your own website. No coding required. In under an hour." Reader thinks "wow, didn't know that was possible" and keeps scrolling. Works when the product delivers a clearly-better result on a known dimension (speed, cost, quality, scope).
- **Address the top buying objection:** Hero preempts the #1 reason visitors bounce. "Build your own website. You don't need to know how to code or hire a designer." Works when visitors already want the category but have a specific fear blocking purchase.

**How to pick:** Survey 10 customers. Ask "what almost stopped you from buying?" If the answers cluster around one objection, use Hook 2. If customers describe the *outcome* as what won them over, use Hook 1.

**Optional third move — Choose Your Own Adventure:** If the product has 2+ distinct personas, offer a segmented path at the top of the page ("I'm a designer" / "I'm a developer" / "I'm a founder") and route each to tailored content below.

---

## Close Patterns (CTAs)

- **Narrative-continuation CTA:** Button copy continues the hero's sentence. Hero: "Learn Spanish in 15 minutes a day." CTA: "Start learning." Hero: "Order groceries in under an hour." CTA: "Find food." Never "Get started" / "Sign up free" / "Request a demo" unless those words literally describe the promised next step.
- **Specific over generic:** "Create my first page" > "Sign up." "See my 5-minute plan" > "Learn more." The button is a micro-promise; keep it concrete.
- **Repeat, don't rephrase:** The CTA above the fold and the CTA after features should use the same verb. Consistency trumps cleverness.
- **Friction-reducer subtext:** Below the button, one line that removes the final objection: "No credit card required." / "Free on up to 3 projects." / "Setup takes 2 minutes." Same font size as nav text, not shouty.

## Devices & Patterns

**Bad-alternative contrast** is the primary conversion device. Every feature implicitly compares to the status quo the visitor is currently suffering: "design agencies take weeks and cost $30K; you build it yourself in an afternoon." State the contrast plainly — don't make the visitor do the math.

**Specific-outcome substitution.** Replace every adjective with a measurable outcome. Not "fast" → "under 60 seconds." Not "affordable" → "$19/mo." Not "powerful" → "replaces 4 tools." If the product doesn't have specifics yet, find them before writing copy.

**Objection-handling in-line.** When a feature would trigger the visitor's "yeah but…" reflex, address it in the same paragraph. "Build sites visually. **Without sacrificing the control and customization developers expect.**" The objection and its answer live together.

**Product-in-action imagery.** Every feature image shows the product doing the thing, not abstract illustration. Blur text in screenshots so the eye goes to the *motion* of the product (Julian's explicit rule). No stock photos of handshakes or diverse-looking teams gazing at laptops.

**De-risk the click.** Visitor is deciding whether to spend 2 minutes on your product. Make the next step feel free, short, and reversible. "Free forever on up to 3 projects." / "2-minute setup, no credit card." / "Cancel anytime."

## Syntax & Mechanics

- **Sentence length:** Hero: 6–14 words per sentence. Subheader: 15–30 words total. Features: 8–18 words per sentence, 3 sentences max per paragraph. Fragment sentences everywhere for rhythm ("No coding." / "Free forever." / "In under an hour.").
- **Punctuation:** Em dashes for feature labels and inline asides. Periods over commas — break long sentences into two. Question marks for the very occasional rhetorical setup ("Why pay for a design agency?") but use sparingly. No semicolons. Max one exclamation mark on the entire page.
- **Emphasis:** **Bold** for the value prop label at the start of each feature, and for load-bearing specifics in the subheader ("**1 hour**," "**$19/mo**"). Italics almost never — too subtle for homepage scanning.
- **Emoji:** None. This is a sales page, not a blog post.
- **Numbers:** Always digits. Always specific. "$19/mo" not "affordable." "10,000+ teams" not "thousands of teams." "Under 60 seconds" not "fast setup."
- **Edge:** Clean, no profanity, no "VCs are lazy"-style callouts. The bluntness shows in what's *not* said (no fluff, no hedging) rather than in provocation.
- **Hyperlinks:** Only functional — nav, CTAs, footer, the occasional in-feature "see how it works" link. No citation hyperlinks like the handbook.

## Vocab & Phrases

**Lean into:**
- Specific numbers — time ("under 60 seconds," "in 5 minutes"), money ("$19/mo," "1/500th the cost"), count ("10,000+ teams," "3-click setup")
- Category replacement language — "replaces [tool]," "without [painful alternative]," "instead of [bad status quo]"
- Outcome verbs — "build," "launch," "ship," "find," "start," "see"
- Plain product descriptors — "website design tool," "grocery delivery," "team chat" (state the category, then differentiate)
- Concrete qualifiers — "in 1 hour," "no coding," "without hiring," "for free"
- Named customers / logos — "Used by teams at [Company A], [Company B], and [Company C]"

**Avoid:**
- "Leverage" / "unlock" / "supercharge" / "empower" / "transform"
- "Revolutionary" / "game-changing" / "disruptive" / "next-generation"
- "World-class" / "best-in-class" / "industry-leading" / "cutting-edge"
- "Seamless" / "robust" / "holistic" / "powerful" / "intuitive"
- "The future of X" / "Reimagining X" / "X, reinvented"
- Slogan-style headers ("Workflow, unlocked.") — Julian's explicit anti-pattern
- Vague social proof ("trusted by industry leaders," "loved by thousands")
- Generic CTAs ("Get Started," "Learn More," "Contact Sales") when a narrative-continuation verb would convert better
- Vernacular cameos ("am i rite," "dat FOMO") — works in blog voice, kills homepage trust
- Coined terms unless they're the product name — visitors aren't here for your taxonomy

## Do / Don't

| Do | Don't |
|---|---|
| Describe exactly what you sell in the header — pass the "read only this" test | Write a slogan ("Supercharge your workflow!") that doesn't say what the product is |
| Pick ONE hook: a bold specific claim OR the top objection addressed | Stuff the hero with multiple value props, frameworks, or taxonomies |
| Use named customers/logos in threes for social proof | Write "trusted by thousands" or "loved by industry leaders" |
| Start each feature with a **bolded value-prop label** + em dash | Use plain paragraphs or generic bullets with no visual hierarchy |
| Handle the top objection in the same paragraph it would arise | Save all objections for a separate FAQ below the fold |
| Make CTA copy continue the hero's sentence ("Find food" / "Start learning") | Use generic CTAs ("Get Started," "Sign Up Free," "Learn More") |
| Replace adjectives with measurable outcomes ("under 60s" / "$19/mo") | Rely on adjectives ("fast," "affordable," "powerful") |
| Show product-in-action imagery with text blurred so motion leads the eye | Use stock photos or abstract illustrations disconnected from the product |
| Repeat the CTA above the fold AND after features, with identical copy | Invent a new CTA verb on every button |
| Add friction-reducing subtext under the CTA ("No credit card required") | Leave the button standalone when a 5-word reassurance would lift CR |

## Homepage Section-by-Section Guidance

**Hero — Header (10–14 words):**
- Must be descriptive. Litmus test: if a visitor reads only this line, do they know what you sell?
- Pair with ONE hook: bold specific claim OR top objection addressed.
- No adjectives where a number will do.

**Hero — Subheader (15–30 words, 1–2 sentences):**
- Answer one of: *how does this actually work?* OR *what makes the header's claim believable?*
- Bold the 1–2 specific details that carry the credibility ("In **under 5 minutes**," "For **$19/mo**").

**Hero — CTA:**
- Button verb continues the hero's sentence, not "Get Started."
- One line of friction-reducing subtext below ("No credit card required.").

**Social Proof:**
- 5–8 customer logos OR one impressive count ("Used by 10,000+ teams") — never both in the same strip.
- Place immediately under the hero, before features.

**Features (3–6 blocks, each with):**
- **Bolded value-prop label** — em dash — 1–3 sentence paragraph handling the likely objection for that value prop.
- Product-in-action image demonstrating the value prop. Blur secondary text.
- Each feature laddering back to the hero's primary value prop — not introducing new themes.

**Repeat CTA:**
- Same verb as hero CTA.
- Optional: one-line summary of the hero's promise above the button for visitors who scrolled long.

**Footer:**
- Links only. No marketing copy in the footer.

## Feedback & Testing Checklist (from Julian's guide)

Before shipping, pass the page to two reviewer types — people *in* your market and people *not in* your market. Have both rate:

- **Conversion** — Would you sign up now? If not, what would you need to see?
- **Interest** — Rate 1–10 how well the page sustained interest. What would you cut or rewrite?
- **Clarity** — What's unclear? What questions are you left with?
- **Expansion** — What made you want more detail?
- **Brevity** — If you had to cut half the copy and imagery, what goes?
- **Disbelief** — What triggered your "scammy sales-speak" reflex?

If the page fails Clarity or Disbelief with non-market reviewers, the homepage isn't ready — rewrite before optimizing anything else.

## Adaptation Notes

This style card is a **compressed, homepage-specific variant** of `writing-style-card-julian-shapiro.md`. The parent card optimizes for long-form handbook teaching; this card optimizes for visitor-to-signup conversion on a single page.

**Carried over from parent Julian style:**
- Specificity discipline — numbers over adjectives, named companies over "industry leaders"
- Bolded-label + em dash + description list format — maps directly to feature blocks
- Fragment rhythm — still the core scannability move
- Show-the-math for ROI claims — still the credibility shortcut
- Unhedged assertions — no softeners, no "might help you consider"
- Descriptive-header discipline — Julian's own landing-pages guide is the source

**Dropped for homepage context:**
- **Taxonomies.** The instinct to enumerate 3–7 categories is the single biggest handbook-to-homepage risk. Hero = one message, not a framework. Kill this reflex first.
- **Coined terms.** Unless the coined term *is* your product name, the homepage isn't the place to teach new vocabulary. Visitors won't invest.
- **First-person credential anchors.** "At my fund" / "while running Demand Curve" is authority-building for a handbook byline. On a homepage, the visitor doesn't know you yet; lead with the product, not the founder.
- **Vernacular cameos.** "Foster dat FOMO, am i rite" is a trust-earned-over-3000-words move. On a homepage it reads as unprofessional to a first-time visitor.
- **Investor-perspective coda, numbered Recap, "Next —" segue.** All handbook scaffolding. Homepage closes with a CTA, not a summary.
- **2,000–4,000 word length.** The entire page should be shorter than a single handbook section.

**Adjusted / watch for:**
- **Tone gets slightly more neutral.** Julian's handbook bluntness ("VCs are lazy," "you're being lazy") is edgier than optimal for a first-visitor sales page. Keep the directness, dial back the provocation.
- **Bolded-label format stays, but for features only.** Don't use it in the hero — the hero is one continuous sentence/thought, not a labeled list.
- **Named-examples-in-threes migrates from body copy to social proof strip.** Same rule ("three real named companies"), different page section.
- **Show-the-math still applies — but as a subheader detail, not a paragraph.** "Replaces $30K design agency work" > a full arithmetic walkthrough on the homepage.
