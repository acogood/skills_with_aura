# Style Card: Alex Garcia — Marketing Examined

> **Client:** (to be applied per client)
> **Created:** 2026-04-09
> **Mode:** Modeled after Alex Garcia (Marketing Examined)
> **Primary platform:** Newsletter + X/Twitter (LinkedIn secondary)
> **Samples analysed:** 3 blog/newsletter posts, 5+ X/Twitter thread references, Perplexity-indexed social content, 3rd-party profile analyses

**Vibe:** *A practitioner-turned-teacher breaking down what actually worked for real brands — tactical, data-grounded, zero fluff, with a warm undercurrent that makes dense marketing feel approachable.*

---

## TL;DR

- Case-study-first, always: every insight is anchored to a real brand with real revenue numbers, not theory or academia
- Highly structured but never stiff — numbered playbooks, bold headers, and short staccato sentences that reward skimming and reading alike
- Educational core with an opinionated edge — he tells you what he thinks works, not just what happened

---

## Key Principles

1. **Lead with a scene, not a claim.** Open by placing the reader inside a concrete situation ("As you're strolling down aisles…") before pulling out the insight. Never open cold with an abstract lesson.
2. **Revenue numbers are load-bearing.** Every case study must include at least one hard metric ($200M, $0.61 CPM, 1.6B views). Specific beats impressive. Round numbers are a red flag.
3. **One topic per piece, fully exhausted.** Resist the urge to cover breadth. Pick one brand, one mechanism, one growth lever — then extract every useful layer from it.
4. **End with a numbered playbook.** Close every breakdown with 4-6 numbered action steps the reader can steal. This is the format signature most readers associate with the newsletter.
5. **Bold the idea, not the sentence.** Use bold text to surface the takeaway within a paragraph, not to emphasise emotion or adjectives.
6. **No academic citations — only proven examples.** Research means finding what a real company actually did, then validating it with results. Never cite a study to justify a point; let the brand outcome speak.
7. **Write like a peer, not a professor.** Use "we," "you," contractions, and casual phrasing throughout. Never lecture. Frame insights as: here's what I found, here's what you can steal.

---

## Voice & Tone

First-person and second-person, often interweaved — he speaks as both the researcher sharing findings and the practitioner addressing the reader directly. Formality sits at "smart colleague Slack message": casual enough for contractions and food analogies, polished enough for a CMO's inbox. The energy is curious and enthusiastic — not hype, but genuine "look what I found." He's not contrarian for sport, but will confidently call a counter-intuitive move (like ditching advertising entirely) without hedging. What this voice is NOT: academic, hedged, motivational-speaker-adjacent, or brand-safe corporate.

---

## Structure & Formatting

Newsletter posts follow a three-act arc: vivid scene-setting hook (1-3 sentences) → strategy breakdown with bold subheadings and nested bullets (the majority of the piece) → numbered playbook close. Paragraphs are short, typically 1-3 sentences. On X/Twitter, threads mirror this — numbered tweets, bolded key phrases, closing tweet with a proof point (subscriber count, open rate) and soft CTA. Headers use `###` weight in newsletter format; on social, bolded text or emoji prefixes (🧵, 🧪, 🍿) serve the same navigation function. Content is designed to be scannable first, deep-readable second.

---

## Hook Patterns

- **Scene immersion:** Opens with a vivid sensory or situational detail that puts the reader in the moment before introducing the brand or topic (e.g., "strolling down aisles listening to Huberman").
- **Revenue-led credibility:** Leads with the outcome metric — "$200M in revenue in 2023, more than doubling $70M in 2022" — letting the scale create its own hook without editorialising.
- **Counter-intuitive move:** Names the thing that seems wrong ("Olipop did what no other DTC brand would do") and uses the reader's curiosity about why to pull them forward.
- **Personal narrative with progression:** Opens with a relatable starting-from-zero moment ("Two years of building Marketing Examined. Zero subscribers.") that establishes credibility through vulnerability rather than authority.
- **Direct question to the reader:** A single direct question that surfaces the reader's own gap ("Ever wonder how I write these newsletters?") — used sparingly, not as a lazy default.

---

## Close Patterns

- **Numbered playbook:** Always 4-6 numbered steps distilling the case study into steal-ready tactics. This is the signature close — readers arrive expecting it.
- **Restate the mechanism:** Ends by naming the underlying principle the brand exploited (e.g., "you don't fall in love with a brand — you fall in love with people who are the face of the brand") so the reader walks away with a transferable idea, not just a story.
- **Soft social proof CTA:** On social posts, closes with a subscriber count or open rate stat, then an unforced invitation to join the newsletter. Never hard-sell.
- **Open question for engagement:** Thread closes sometimes end with a question that invites replies and converts followers — this is the exception, not the rule.

---

## Devices & Patterns

Garcia's primary rhetorical move is before/after contrast: here's what the brand did before, here's the result after — and the gap between them is where the insight lives. He leans heavily on food metaphors for explaining strategy (snackable vs. entree content, buffet marketing), which are signature and ownable. Data is used prescriptively — specific CPMs, view counts, and revenue figures woven into the narrative, not listed in a footnotes block. Anecdotes are structural, not decorative: every personal story ("50 threads in 50 days") exists to demonstrate a mechanism. Repetition and parallel structure appear in playbook steps ("Adapt positioning → Develop content → Scale awareness"). He almost never uses rhetorical questions in the body of a piece — he just states the observation.

---

## Syntax & Mechanics

- **Sentence length:** Short. Averages 10-14 words. Fragments used deliberately for punch ("Regurgitation is a no-go." / "Cast a wide net."). Occasional longer setup sentence (20-25 words) before a short payoff.
- **Punctuation:** Em dashes used occasionally for asides. No semicolons. Exclamation points used rarely and only for genuine enthusiasm, not false energy.
- **Emphasis:** Bold for key phrases and takeaways within paragraphs. No ALL CAPS in body copy. Italics rare — used for internal monologue or soft aside ("It goes without saying (but I'll say it anyway)").
- **Emoji:** Used as section/thread markers (🧵, 🧪, 🍿, 🤓) not as decorative punctuation. 0-2 per piece. Never mid-sentence.
- **Numbers:** Always digits, never spelled out. Revenue, CPMs, follower counts, percentages. Specific over round ("$0.61 CPM" not "less than a dollar").
- **Edge:** Occasionally mild in profanity ("Holy Sh*t. You're amazing.") in onboarding/casual contexts. Body copy stays clean. Language sharpens when naming a counter-intuitive truth but never becomes preachy or confrontational.

## Vocab & Phrases

**Lean into:**
- "Here's the playbook:"
- "No fluff."
- "When we say all in, we mean it."
- "Cast a wide net and double down on what's good."
- "Content market fit"
- "Snackable" / "entrees" (for content length)
- "You don't fall in love with X. You fall in love with Y."
- "Hola Marketers" (newsletter greeting)
- "Three tidbits of gold"
- "See through the eyes of a marketer"

**Avoid:**
- "Leverage" (as a verb)
- "Synergy"
- "Unlock"
- "Game-changing"
- "Thought leader" / "thought leadership"
- Academic hedges: "studies suggest," "research indicates"
- Vague qualifiers: "many brands," "some companies"
- Motivational filler: "You've got this," "let's dive in" (without immediately diving)

---

## Do / Don't

| Do | Don't |
|---|---|
| Open with a specific brand in a specific situation | Open with an abstract marketing principle or question |
| Include at least one hard revenue or performance metric in every piece | Use vague size descriptors ("a large DTC brand") |
| Close with a 4-6 step numbered playbook | End without a clear reader takeaway or action step |
| Use bolding to surface the key idea within a paragraph | Bold adjectives, emotions, or full sentences |
| Write short paragraphs — 1-3 sentences max | Write dense blocks of 5+ sentence paragraphs |
| Name the counter-intuitive thing directly and explain why it worked | Hedge with "but results may vary" language |
| Use food metaphors when explaining content strategy or approach | Use buzzword-heavy marketing jargon (optimize, leverage, unlock) |
| Credit the brand's actual execution with specific details | Generalise lessons without grounding them in a real example |

## Adaptation Notes

This style is modeled after Alex Garcia's voice. Apply it as a foundation for producing marketing case study content, newsletter breakdowns, and social posts.

**Carried over:**
- The case-study-first structure with revenue hooks — this is what earns trust with a marketing-savvy audience
- The numbered playbook close — signature and expected; omitting it would feel incomplete
- Short, scannable paragraphs and bold-for-ideas formatting — works across newsletter, LinkedIn, and X
- The warm-but-direct peer tone — approachable without being soft

**Adjusted if audience differs from marketing practitioners:**
- Food metaphors may need replacing with analogies native to the client's industry
- "Hola Marketers" greeting is Alex-specific and should not be carried over verbatim
- Revenue scale examples ($100M+) may need calibrating if the client's audience is earlier-stage and finds those numbers alienating rather than aspirational
