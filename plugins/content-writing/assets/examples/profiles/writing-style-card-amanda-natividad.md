# Style Card: Amanda Natividad

> **Client:** —
> **Created:** 2026-04-09
> **Mode:** Modeled after Amanda Natividad (VP Marketing, SparkToro / @amandanat)
> **Primary platform:** Newsletter (The Menu, Substack) + LinkedIn + X/Twitter
> **Samples analysed:** 6 full newsletter posts, 1 SparkToro long-form blog post, Perplexity-indexed quotes and summaries from podcast appearances and social posts

**Vibe:** *Smart practitioner having an honest conversation — opinionated but never preachy, precise but never dry, casual enough to mention California burritos in a marketing newsletter.*

---

## TL;DR

- Leads with a single concrete observation or anecdote, then builds to a reframe — never opens with an abstract claim or a question
- Structurally disciplined: short paragraphs, bold for key terms, clear section headers, but always reserves space for a personal aside or cultural reference that breaks the "content" feel
- Confidently contrarian, but earns it — takes a clear stance, backs it with data or a named example, then lands with a concise transferable lesson

---

## Key Principles

1. **Earn the take before you state it.** Open with a specific scene, data point, or named study. The opinion comes second, not first.
2. **Short paragraphs, always.** Max 3 sentences in a body paragraph. Paragraph breaks are used for rhythm, not just when the thought is complete.
3. **Name things.** Use specific numbers (95% of marketers, 97.3% of Facebook views, 18 months, $150) instead of vague qualifiers like "many" or "most."
4. **Take a side.** Write "Incorrect." not "That's not quite right." Natividad doesn't hedge opinions with softeners; she asserts, then supports.
5. **One joke per piece.** Self-aware humour appears once — a parenthetical, a deadpan aside — then disappears. It's a seasoning, not a main course.
6. **End every main section with a transferable rule, not a summary.** The reader should be able to clip the last sentence of any section and use it standalone.
7. **The newsletter is not just marketing.** The "Other Things" section at the end of each issue is non-negotiable — pop culture, food, a link she liked. It signals she's a whole person, not a content machine.

## Voice & Tone

First person throughout. Writes as a practitioner who also happens to have data — not as an analyst who happens to have opinions. The energy is warm-but-precise: she's clearly thought about this longer than you have, but she's inviting you into the reasoning rather than lecturing you.

Formality sits at "smart Slack message from a VP" — contractions are standard, the occasional "look," "fine," or "no cap" lands without feeling forced. She swears almost never but uses sharp, slightly impolite framing when challenging conventional wisdom ("Misdiagnosing wins as random leads to chasing chaos").

Emotional range is narrow but deliberate: she doesn't perform excitement or urgency. The loudest the voice gets is dry exasperation. The warmest it gets is a genuine aside about bread or a burrito. This restraint makes the moments of warmth land harder.

Not: a thought leader broadcasting insights. Not: a neutral explainer summarising the literature. She's a practitioner reporting from the field, with receipts.

## Structure & Formatting

Newsletter posts run 500–900 words in the main section, with a 150–300 word "Other Things" section appended. Blog posts run 1,000–1,500 words. X/Twitter posts are short, declarative, and stand alone.

Paragraph length: 1–3 sentences. Never 4+. Line breaks are used aggressively — every 1–2 sentences on social, every 2–3 sentences in newsletter prose.

Headers appear in longer pieces (newsletter and blog) and are written as plain declarative statements, not questions. Example: "The Core Tension" not "What's the Core Tension?"

Bold is used sparingly — for a key term on first use, or a stat that's load-bearing. Never full sentences in bold. Italics used for emphasis or internal-monologue asides ("fine: that's B2B sales too").

Lists appear when there are 3+ parallel items that would clutter prose. Each list item is a complete thought, not a fragment.

## Hook Patterns

- **Scene-first:** Opens with a specific observed moment — a post she saw on Threads, an experiment she ran with eight friends, a sourdough starter named JJ — before expanding to the larger pattern. The scene is always concrete: location, names, sensory details.
- **Bold contradiction:** States an assumption the reader holds as fact, immediately follows with a one-word or two-word refutation ("Incorrect." / "Not quite."), then spends the rest of the piece unpacking why.
- **Data surprise:** Opens with a statistic that is either counterintuitively large or counterintuitively small (95% of marketers use AI; the average semantic similarity score was 0.081), then turns it into a question about what it means.
- **Named research anchor:** Leads with a specific study or report (Typeform survey of 2,000+ people; SparkToro + Gumshoe AI research), uses the authority of the source to ground what would otherwise be opinion.
- **Process narrative:** Opens with a personal action she took (buying equipment, running an experiment, rehydrating a sourdough starter) and plays out what happened, treating the experience as a case study.

---

## Close Patterns

- **Transferable rule:** Ends with a single declarative sentence that works as a standalone principle, stripped of the specific example — designed to be screenshot-able or quoted out of context.
- **Reframed question:** Closes by replacing the question the reader started with ("how do we get more clicks?") with a better one ("who on this team is responsible for deciding what's worth doing?").
- **Parenthetical aside:** Appends a dry, self-aware aside in parentheses or italics after the "real" ending — "(Fine: that's B2B sales too.)" or "(Follow for more financial wisdom.)" — diffusing the didactic weight of the lesson.
- **The soft landing + "Other Things":** Every newsletter issue closes the main argument cleanly, then pivots into a clearly labelled personal section (food, pop culture, links) that resets the register entirely. This is structural, not incidental.

---

## Devices & Patterns

Contrast is her primary rhetorical move: old internet vs new internet, effort vs output, fine vs good, clicks vs trust. She sets up a binary, complicates it slightly, then resolves it with a principle.

Data citation is selective and precise. She doesn't cite studies to pad authority — she cites them when the number is genuinely surprising or when it locks in an argument that would otherwise be soft. Every stat includes the source name (SparkToro, Typeform, Ehrenberg-Bass, Meta) not just the number.

Analogy draws from adjacent craft domains — sourdough baking, acting careers (Pedro Pascal's 20-year overnight success), sports — not from tech or business. The analogies feel fresh because they're from outside the expected vocabulary of marketing.

Self-deprecation appears in parenthetical asides, not in the main argument. She undercuts the "lesson" tone just before it tips into preaching: "I calculated: 'make 20 loaves and break even.' (Follow for more financial wisdom.)" The joke signals she's aware of how earnest she's being.

Personal anecdotes are frequent and functional — they're always the source of the insight, not decoration. The experiment she ran with her mom friends is the proof; the sourdough starter is the metaphor; the Threads wedding post is the case study.

## Syntax & Mechanics

- **Sentence length:** Averages 12–18 words in prose. Mixes 3–5 word declarative fragments with 25–30 word setup sentences for rhythm. Fragment sentences used as emphasis, never as filler.
- **Punctuation:** Em dashes used for asides and to create a beat before a pivot. Parentheses for self-deprecating jokes or qualifying asides. No semicolons. Exclamation marks: maximum one per issue, usually in the "Other Things" section.
- **Emphasis:** Bold for key terms and load-bearing stats. Italics for internal monologue, parenthetical irony, or publication titles. No ALL CAPS. No underlining.
- **Emoji:** Present in the "Other Things" section as list markers (🤖, 🎁, 🫣) — one per item. Never in the main argument.
- **Numbers:** Always digits, never spelled out. Percentages over round numbers. Source + number always paired ("Meta's spring 2025 report: 97.3%").
- **Edge:** No profanity. Occasional pointed phrasing when challenging conventional wisdom ("chasing chaos," "fuzzy idea"). Uses Gen Z vernacular sparingly and self-awarely ("No cap." used once, flagged as coming from a Gen Z colleague).

## Vocab & Phrases

**Lean into:**
- "zero-click content" / "zero-click marketing"
- "standalone value"
- "vibes-to-facts ratio" / "balance vibes with facts"
- "the decision happens before the click"
- "build familiarity, trust, and preference"
- "judgment" (as the scarce resource)
- "reps" (repetitions, practice)
- "at SparkToro, we..." (grounds claims in lived institutional experience)
- "look" (as a conversational pivot before a direct assertion)
- "actually good" vs "good enough"

**Avoid:**
- "leverage" / "unlock" / "skyrocket"
- "thought leadership" (she enacts it, never names it)
- "game-changer" / "revolutionary" / "disruption"
- "at the end of the day"
- "circling back" / "double-clicking on"
- "robust" / "holistic" / "synergy"
- Hedging softeners: "might want to consider," "one could argue," "it seems like"
- "content is king"
- Passive voice in instructions

## Do / Don't

| Do | Don't |
|---|---|
| Open with a specific scene, study, or named experiment | Open with a rhetorical question ("Have you ever wondered...?") |
| State the take directly and early, then support it | Build to the opinion as if it's a reveal at the end |
| Cite the source name alongside every statistic | Drop a number without attribution ("studies show...") |
| Use bold for one or two load-bearing terms per section | Bold entire sentences or use it decoratively |
| End the main section with a one-sentence transferable rule | Summarise what you just said ("So in summary...") |
| Add a personal, off-topic aside in a clearly labelled section | Weave pop culture or personal anecdotes throughout the argument |
| Use contrast as your primary structure (old vs new, fine vs good) | Use a problem-agitate-solve funnel structure |
| Let one piece of self-deprecating humour land and move on | Layer irony throughout to seem relatable |
| Name real companies, studies, and people | Speak in abstractions ("some marketers," "a major platform") |
| Write "Incorrect." as a full stop | Write "While there's merit to that view, it's worth considering..." |

## Adaptation Notes

This style is modeled after Amanda Natividad's voice and is intended for use as a writing reference or for content modeled after her approach.

**Carried over:**
- Scene-first opening structure — grounds the argument in something concrete before expanding
- Selective, precise data citation with source names — signals rigor without overwhelming
- The "Other Things" section format — signals full personhood, prevents the content from feeling purely transactional
- Confident assertion followed by support — never buries the lede
- Short paragraph discipline and aggressive line-breaking — maximises scannability

**Adjusted / watch for:**
- SparkToro-specific institutional references ("at SparkToro, we...") don't transfer to other voices — replace with equivalent first-hand experience
- The Gen Z vernacular ("no cap") works because she flags it as someone else's voice; using it unselfconsciously would feel off-brand
- The sourdough-level personal detail works because her newsletter has established a personal register over time — new accounts need to earn that intimacy gradually
- Her data often comes from SparkToro's own proprietary research; without that access, lean on named third-party studies instead
