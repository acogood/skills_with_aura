# Style Card: Katelyn Bourgoin

> **Client:** AI CMO / Diffmode
> **Created:** 2026-04-09
> **Mode:** Modeled after Katelyn Bourgoin (@KateBour)
> **Primary platform:** Newsletter (Why We Buy) + X/Twitter + LinkedIn
> **Samples analysed:** 4 newsletter issues/pages, 6 X/Twitter examples, 3 LinkedIn post examples, 8 secondary source profiles

**Vibe:** *The fun psychology nerd who's done the research so you look smart — direct, slightly conspiratorial, casually profane, never boring.*

---

## TL;DR

- Opens with a vivid "imagine this" scenario or a shocking stat, then names the cognitive bias — never leads with the concept itself.
- Emoji-sectioned newsletter structure (🧠 → 🧐 → 🤑 → 💥) makes every issue scannable and snackable; X/Twitter is tighter but still punchy and line-broken.
- Contrarian and opinionated with light but genuine self-deprecation — will say "horseshit" and "crazy sh*t" without apology, but reserves profanity for emphasis, not decoration.

---

## Key Principles

1. **Lead with the scenario, not the concept.** Anchor every piece in a specific, relatable situation before naming the psychology. "Imagine your prospect nodding along while their brain whispers 'nah.'" Concept labels come second.
2. **Translate, don't cite.** References psychological research (Kahneman, cognitive fluency studies, behavioral economics) but strips out academic language. Cite the idea, not the journal — make it feel like insider knowledge, not homework.
3. **Use specific numbers as credibility tokens.** "1,500+ hours," "2,400 new subscribers," "$14,950 in 57 minutes" — not round numbers. Specificity signals authenticity, not polish.
4. **Be openly opinionated.** State the contrarian take flat-out: "Be everywhere all the time content advice is total horseshit." Don't hedge. The authority comes from certainty, not qualification.
5. **Break lines aggressively on social.** On X and LinkedIn, each sentence or clause gets its own line. Paragraphs of 1–2 sentences max. Reader should never face a wall of text.
6. **Make readers feel smart, not impressed.** The goal is "wow, I'm smarter for having read this" not "wow she's brilliant." Every piece teaches something the reader wants to share at a meeting.
7. **P.S. is a conversion tool.** Newsletter P.S. lines are tiered offers or a "reply [word] for a cheat sheet" mechanic — always action-oriented, never decoration.

## Voice & Tone

First-person throughout, with frequent direct second-person address ("your buyer," "your thumbs," "you're a little annoyed"). The posture is a peer who's done the research so you don't have to — confident without being preachy, expert without being academic. Energy is warm-conspiratorial: she's letting you in on something.

Casual enough for contractions, profanity-when-warranted, and parenthetical asides like "(Katelyn here 👋)". Professional enough that VPs forward her newsletters to their teams. Not corporate, not bro-y — more "sharp marketer girlfriend who reads the studies."

What the voice is NOT: guru-ish, hedgy, abstract, formal, passive. Never "many experts believe." Always "here's what the research shows and here's how to steal it."

## Structure & Formatting

**Newsletter:** Fixed sectioned anatomy with emoji section headers:
- **"Imagine this…" vignette** — 2–4 sentences, present tense, second-person, relatable scenario
- **🧠 The psychology** — names and explains the cognitive concept, 2–4 short paragraphs
- **🧐 Inside your buyer's mind** — applies the concept to purchase decisions specifically
- **🤑 How to apply this** — 3 numbered tactics, each 1–3 sentences
- **💥 The short of it** — 1–2 sentence takeaway for sharing

Newsletter paragraphs: 1–3 sentences, rarely 4. Heavy use of bold for key phrases. Italics for reader's inner monologue or buyer's inner monologue.

**Social (X/LinkedIn):** Single-sentence or two-sentence units, each on its own line. Threads layer complexity gradually — hook tweet stands alone, body tweets build on it. No bullet walls on X. LinkedIn tolerates numbered lists more than X does.

Typical X post length: 5–12 line-broken sentences for threads; 1–4 sentences for standalone takes. Emoji used sparingly on X (0–2 per tweet), more liberally as section markers in newsletter.

## Hook Patterns

- **"Imagine this…" scenario:** Opens in present tense, second person, dropping reader into a relatable buying or marketing situation. "Imagine your prospect nodding along but their brain whispers 'nah.'" Names the feeling before naming the bias.
- **Shocking stat as entry point:** A counterintuitive or dramatic number lands first, then the question: "Your thumbs travel two marathons a year scrolling — so what makes someone stop?" Statistic is the hook, the concept is the payoff.
- **Contrarian declaration:** States the unpopular truth flat-out in sentence one. "'Be everywhere all the time' is horseshit." No warm-up. No setup. Provocation as the entry point.
- **"Most people think X, but actually Y":** Flips the conventional wisdom, usually in two lines. Sets up a before/after contrast and positions the reader as someone about to get smarter.
- **Personal or business audit reveal:** "I audited my business and found 3 high-leverage assets." First-person credibility hook with a number. Draws the reader in by modeling the behavior she's about to teach.
- **Specific dollar/time claim:** "$14,950 in 57 minutes with two emails and a Google Doc." Specificity makes it feel real, not braggadocious. Sets up "here's the psychology that made it work."

---

## Close Patterns

- **The tiered P.S.:** Ends newsletter with 3 progressively deeper offerings — free resource → mid-tier product → premium access. Always actionable. "Reply [word]" mechanic used to boost replies and segment the audience.
- **Single-sentence takeaway:** Distills the whole piece into one quotable line for sharing. Written to travel — the reader should be able to forward it in a Slack message.
- **Action directive + psychology callback:** "Next time your prospect says 'let me think about it,' remember: that's normalcy bias talking. Try [tactic] instead." Loops back to the opening scenario, closes the loop.
- **Permission to steal:** "You can use this in your next campaign. Go steal it." Positions the reader as capable of applying what they just learned. Removes hesitation.
- **Engagement question (light):** Occasional soft question at end of X posts: "What's the sneakiest bias you've spotted in the wild?" Not a hard CTA, just an invitation. Used selectively, not on every post.

## Devices & Patterns

Contrast is the engine: old way vs. new way, "see the light" vs. "feel the heat," standard marketing advice vs. what the psychology actually says. Nearly every piece is built around one flip.

Data usage: cites studies and researchers by name (Kahneman, cognitive fluency research, behavioral economics) but translates immediately into practitioner language. No percentages from academic studies in the classic sense — she'll use business metrics (conversion rates, subscriber counts, revenue figures) as proof points. Sample sizes and p-values: absent. Named effects and well-known studies: present.

Personal anecdotes used selectively as openers or credibility grounding — bankruptcy story, sick baby tweeting, "I audited my business" — not as the main content. Vulnerability is strategic, not constant.

Analogy domain: psychology concepts mapped onto everyday consumer scenarios (keto tea friend, Grey Poupon, Mute Snoring). Brands used as real-world proof, not abstract examples.

## Syntax & Mechanics

- **Sentence length:** Short by default — 8–15 words. Mixes fragments ("People change when they feel the heat.") with occasional longer setup sentences (20–25 words) before a short payoff.
- **Punctuation:** Em dashes for asides and pivots. Parentheses for personality injections "(Katelyn here 👋)". Ellipsis for narrative pause "Let's get into it…". No semicolons.
- **Emphasis:** Bold for key concepts and contrarian claims. Italics for reader/buyer inner monologue. Not both — picks one. Never ALL CAPS.
- **Emoji:** In newsletter: emoji as section headers (🧠 🧐 🤑 💥), 1–3 per section divider, not inline decoration. On X: 0–2 per post, used for tonal punctuation (😅 for self-deprecation, 👀 for teasing). On LinkedIn: slightly more, still restrained.
- **Numbers:** Always digits. Specific over round ("57 minutes" not "under an hour," "2,400 subscribers" not "thousands"). Revenue and subscriber figures cited regularly.
- **Edge:** Casual profanity lands occasionally for emphasis ("horseshit," "crazy sh*t"). Never gratuitous — reserved for genuine emphasis on a contrarian point. Clean elsewhere.

## Vocab & Phrases

**Lean into:**
- "why people buy" / "buying triggers"
- "pain-killing promise"
- "feel the heat" (vs. "see the light")
- "un-ignorable" / "thumb-stopping"
- "whoever gets closer to the customer wins"
- "marketing geeks"
- "irrational monkey brain"
- "steal [the tactic / the weapon / this]"
- "inside the buyer's mind"
- "chocolate-covered almond" (for content strategy)

**Avoid:**
- "leverage" / "unlock" / "supercharge"
- "game-changing" / "revolutionary"
- "thought leadership"
- "synergy" / "alignment"
- "deep dive" (she does them but doesn't call them that)
- "content strategy" (talks tactics, not strategy labels)
- Academic citation format ("Journal of Consumer Research, 2019")
- Passive voice constructions ("it has been shown that")

## Do / Don't

| Do | Don't |
|---|---|
| Open with a specific scenario before naming the psychology concept | Lead with the concept name or definition |
| Use emoji as structural section headers in longer pieces | Scatter emoji randomly throughout for decoration |
| State contrarian opinions flat-out in sentence one | Hedge with "some people might argue" or "it depends" |
| Cite named psychological effects and researchers; translate immediately | Use academic citation format or include sample sizes/p-values |
| Use specific numbers: "$14,950," "57 minutes," "2,400 subscribers" | Use round numbers or vague qualifiers ("many," "most," "often") |
| End with a tiered P.S. or a single quotable takeaway sentence | End with a generic "let me know your thoughts!" or no close at all |
| Break lines aggressively on social — 1–2 sentences per paragraph | Write paragraph blocks on X or LinkedIn |
| Name the psychological bias or effect with confidence | Say "some research suggests" or bury the insight |
| Use casual profanity selectively to land a contrarian point | Swear for style or across every post |
| Make the reader feel smart and able to apply this immediately | Make the reader feel impressed by your expertise |

## Adaptation Notes

This style is modeled after Katelyn Bourgoin's voice, adapted for use in marketing content for the AI CMO client context.

**Carried over:**
- The emoji-sectioned newsletter anatomy (🧠/🧐/🤑/💥) — strong structural signature that makes issues scannable
- The "imagine this" scenario hook as primary opening move — proven for buyer psychology content
- Contrarian, opinionated tone with flat-out declarations rather than hedged claims
- Translate-don't-cite approach to research: name the effect, skip the footnote
- Aggressive line-breaking on social posts
- Specific numbers over round numbers as credibility signals
- P.S. mechanic with tiered offerings

**Adjusted:**
- Profanity kept but used even more selectively — the client context may span conservative enterprise buyers; reserve it for the highest-impact contrarian moments only
- Personal vulnerability anchors (bankruptcy story, sick baby tweeting) are Katelyn's stories — replace with client-specific or generic practitioner scenarios
- "Marketing geeks" in-group language should be adapted to match the specific audience segment (e.g., "growth teams," "first marketing hires" depending on the profile in use)
- Academic research translation should lean on studies relevant to B2B SaaS buying behavior specifically, not just consumer psychology
